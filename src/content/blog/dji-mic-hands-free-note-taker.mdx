Most meeting note takers send a bot into your call. It shows up in the
participant list, your audio goes off to someone's server, and a summary comes
back later. That's a lot of machinery for writing down what people said.

You can do all of it on your own Mac. The DJI mic records,
[MacParakeet](https://macparakeet.com) turns it into text, and nothing leaves
the machine.

There's also a trick hiding in the mic itself. That little link button on the
transmitter already sends a real keypress to your Mac over USB. So the mic on
your shirt can start and stop the recording, and you never touch the laptop.
That's the second half of this post, and honestly it's the part that makes the
whole thing worth setting up.

## What you get

Clip the mic on. Tap the button. Talk. Tap it again. The text shows up wherever
your cursor is, or you get a full meeting transcript with a summary. No bot in
the call, no audio leaving your Mac, and your hands stay where they are.

## What you need

| You need | Why |
| --- | --- |
| An Apple Silicon Mac | MacParakeet wants M1 or newer, on macOS 14.2 or later. |
| A DJI wireless mic | Any of them work as a mic. For the button trick, I've confirmed the 1st generation (`AST01` transmitter, `ASR01` receiver) and the Mic Mini 2. Step 1 of part three tells you about yours in ten seconds. |
| The USB-C cable from the box | It has to be a data cable. A charge-only one is the number one reason the receiver never shows up. |
| [Karabiner-Elements](https://karabiner-elements.pqrs.org) 15.3.18 or newer | The app that catches the button press. Only needed for part three. Older versions don't have the `is_consumer` setting this depends on. |
| [karabiner-human-config](https://github.com/nrjdalal/karabiner-human-config) | Strongly recommended. Karabiner's own config format is brutal, and this lets you write it in a few readable lines instead. [More on it below.](#a-nicer-way-to-live-with-karabinerjson) |
| Homebrew | For one install command. |

## Part one: the mic

### Switch the receiver to Mono

Do this first. It's the setting people miss, and it's annoying to spot later.

In **Stereo** mode, the receiver sends transmitter 1 to the left channel and
transmitter 2 to the right. If you're using one mic, half your recording is
silence. It sounds like the audio is stuck in one ear, and you're handing the
transcriber less than you could.

**Mono** puts everything in one channel. That's what you want for speech.

- **Mic 2 and Mic 3**: on the receiver's touchscreen, swipe down or tap the
  gear, then Recording Mode, then Mono.
- **Mic Mini**: there's no screen, so set it in the DJI Mimo app on your phone.

Ignore Safety Track. It records a quieter backup copy, which is handy for video
editing and does nothing for you here.

### Plug it in and pick it

Connect the receiver with the USB-C cable, then open **System Settings**,
**Sound**, **Input**. You'll see a new device, usually called
`Wireless Microphone RX`. Click it.

Don't use the receiver's 3.5 mm jack into the Mac instead. A MacBook's
headphone port expects a headset plug, so you'll get bad audio or none at all.

### Set the gain on the receiver

While you're in that Sound panel, talk normally and watch the input meter. You
want it bouncing around two thirds of the way across.

Too quiet and you're transcribing room noise along with your voice. Loud enough
to peg the meter and you get distortion, which turns into nonsense words that no
amount of AI cleanup will fix.

Turn the gain on the DJI receiver itself, not in macOS. You'll get a cleaner
signal than you would by boosting a quiet recording afterwards.

## Part two: local transcription

### Install MacParakeet

```bash
brew install --cask macparakeet
```

It's free and open source. The transcription runs on NVIDIA's Parakeet model,
built to run on Apple Silicon, so it really is happening on your Mac and not
behind someone's API.

### First launch

Open it. macOS might mention it came from the internet, which is one click since
the app is notarized.

It'll download about 600 MB, which is the speech model plus speaker detection.
That happens once. After that you can transcribe with the wifi off.

### The permissions

| Permission | What it's for | Do you need it? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Microphone | Hearing the mic | Yes, always |
| Accessibility | Global hotkey and pasting text | Yes, for the button |
| Screen Recording | Getting the other person's voice on a call | Only for online meetings |
| Calendar | Auto-starting on scheduled meetings | Optional |

<Callout type="note" title="Why it asks for Screen Recording">
  macOS has no separate permission for recording system sound. If an app wants
  the other side of a Zoom call, it has to ask for Screen Recording, because
  that's the only door there is. It isn't watching your screen. If you only
  record in-person conversations, skip it. Mic-only mode never asks.
</Callout>

### Point it at the mic

In Settings, set the input to `Wireless Microphone RX`. If your version doesn't
have a device picker, it just follows whatever macOS is set to, which you
already sorted out.

Then pick how much you want it to hear:

- **Mic only**, for meetings in a room and notes to yourself. Doesn't need
  Screen Recording.
- **Mic plus system audio**, for calls. Your voice comes from the DJI mic and
  everyone else's comes from your Mac's audio. Nothing joins the call and nobody
  sees a recorder in the participant list.

### Summaries, if you want them

Transcription works on its own. Turning a transcript into notes needs a model,
and you've got two reasonable options.

Point it at a cloud model (Anthropic, OpenAI, Gemini, OpenRouter) and you'll get
better summaries. Only the text gets sent, never the audio. An hour of talking
is maybe 10 to 15 thousand tokens, so it costs pennies.

Or run it locally with Ollama or LM Studio. The summaries aren't as good, but
nothing leaves your Mac at all. On 16 GB of RAM, stick to 8B models.

That's the setup working. Everything after this is about not having to touch the
laptop.

## Part three: the button

DJI describes the link button as a camera shutter remote for your phone, and
says nothing about computers. But here's the thing: phone cameras take a photo
when you press volume up. So a shutter remote is really just a gadget that sends
volume presses. Plug the receiver into a Mac and those turn up as a normal
keyboard event.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Check your mic actually sends something">
    Plug in the receiver, make sure the transmitter is on and paired, and tap the
    link button once.

    <VolumeHudSketch />

    If the volume popup appears, you're set. Everything below will work.

    If nothing happens, open Karabiner-EventViewer, go to the Devices tab, and
    look for the receiver. If it isn't listed, your model only presents itself as
    an audio device and there's nothing to catch.

    <Callout type="warning" title="Tap it, don't hold it">
      Holding the link button starts pairing. You'll see the green light on the
      transmitter go from steady to blinking, and if it finishes you'll have to
      pair the two units again before the mic works. Quick taps only.
    </Callout>
  </Step>

  <Step title="Get the device ids">
    Karabiner makes a note of every device it sees, so your ids are already in
    its config file. Read them instead of guessing:

    <Terminal>{`$ python3 -c "
import json, os
d = json.load(open(os.path.expanduser('~/.config/karabiner/karabiner.json')))
for dev in d['profiles'][0].get('devices', []):
    print(dev.get('identifiers'), 'ignore=', dev.get('ignore'))
"
{'is_consumer': True, 'product_id': 16392, 'vendor_id': 11427} ignore= False`}</Terminal>

    Vendor `11427` is DJI. The product id changes between models: mine is `16392`
    on the 1st generation, and the Mic Mini 2 comes up as `16401`. Use whatever
    yours says.

    `ignore= False` is good news. It means Karabiner is already watching the
    device and you don't have to switch anything on.
  </Step>

  <Step title="Turn the volume press into F13">
    Open `karabiner.json`, in `~/.config/karabiner/`, find your receiver under
    `profiles[0].devices`, and add a `simple_modifications` array:

    ```json
    {
      "identifiers": { "vendor_id": 11427, "product_id": 16392, "is_consumer": true },
      "ignore": false,
      "simple_modifications": [
        { "from": { "consumer_key_code": "volume_increment" }, "to": [{ "key_code": "f13" }] },
        { "from": { "consumer_key_code": "volume_decrement" }, "to": [{ "key_code": "f13" }] }
      ]
    }
    ```

    Mine only ever sent `volume_increment`, but some receivers might alternate
    between up and down, so the second line is cheap insurance.

    You can do the same thing in the app if you'd rather not edit JSON. The
    Simple Modifications tab has a device dropdown at the top, and choosing your
    receiver there writes exactly this. And if hand-editing this file is putting
    you off generally, there's a much nicer way to manage it further down.

    <KarabinerDeviceSketch />

    Karabiner picks up the change straight away, so there's nothing to restart.
    **To check it worked: tap the button and the volume popup should be gone.**
  </Step>

  <Step title="Tell MacParakeet to listen for F13">
    Settings, Capture, Dictation. There are two hotkey rows and only one of them
    can work with this mic.

    <DictationHotkeySketch />

    Hit **Change** on the hands-free row, then tap the link button. It'll come up
    as F13.

    There's a nice accident in this. A MacBook's function row stops at F12, so
    there's no way to type F13 on the keyboard, which makes the DJI button the
    only thing you own that can put it in that box. The button you're setting up
    is also the only way to set it up.
  </Step>

  <Step title="Make sure it really saved">
    Worth checking, because the settings window will happily show you a label
    that isn't what got stored. MacParakeet keeps the hotkey as JSON inside a
    binary blob, so a plain `defaults read` just gives you hex:

    <Terminal>{`$ defaults export com.macparakeet.MacParakeet - | grep -A2 hotkeyTrigger
{"kind":"keyCode","keyCode":105}
# 105 is F13. If you see "kind":"modifier" it quietly stayed on Fn.`}</Terminal>

    Then go talk at something. Open any text box, tap the button, say a sentence,
    tap again.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## Why not just use Fn

This was my first idea, and it's the obvious one, since double-tapping Fn is
already how you start dictation on a Mac.

It doesn't work, and the reason is worth knowing even if you never own a DJI
mic. **Any Karabiner rule that touches Fn kills the built-in double-tap.** The
Globe key gets handled so low down that a fake press can't reproduce the
gesture. So remapping Fn breaks dictation, and mapping something else *to* Fn
runs into the same wall from the other side.

If you've already got a rule involving Fn, system dictation is quietly broken on
your Mac right now, and that's why.

F13 dodges all of it. Nothing on the keyboard sends it, so nothing else wants
it, and you don't have to change any config you already have. Something like
`fn+space` is fine too. It's only the bare double-tap that breaks.

## Why you can't hold it down

The obvious wish is push to talk: hold the button while you speak, let go when
you're done. You can't, and I'd rather tell you now than have you spend an
evening on it.

The link button does two things and that's it. Tap sends the shutter press. Hold
starts pairing. There's no in-between state where the button is being held, so
there's nothing for Karabiner to hold down either.

What you want instead is auto-stop after silence, which MacParakeet has and most
others do too. Tap once, say your bit, stop talking, and it stops on its own.
Same feeling, without holding anything.

## A nicer way to live with karabiner.json

Raw `karabiner.json` is rough to maintain by hand. One remap is 170-odd lines of
nested JSON, and you end up scrolling through it every time you want to change a
key.

[karabiner-human-config](https://github.com/nrjdalal/karabiner-human-config) is
the fix. You write a small readable file instead:

```json
{
  "caps": { "t": "hyper", "a": "100 caps" },
  "rcmd": {
    "_self": "rcmd",
    "w": "$ open \"https://web.whatsapp.com\""
  }
}
```

then run `npx karabiner-human-config` and it compiles that into the real thing.
Caps lock as hyper, right command plus W to open WhatsApp, app-specific groups,
shell commands. If you have more than a couple of remaps, it's worth the swap.

Two things to know if you use it, and they're why the rule above goes in
`devices[]` rather than the more obvious spot.

**A complex modification gets wiped.** These tools tend to rebuild
`profiles[0].complex_modifications.rules` from scratch every run. I tried it on a
throwaway config first: `devices[]` and `global` came through untouched, and a
hand-written complex rule was gone. Put it in `devices[]` and it survives.

**This one rule has to stay outside it.** Karabiner needs `consumer_key_code`
instead of `key_code` for a volume event, and the generator writes `{ key_code }`
every time, so there's no way to express it in the short file. Not a problem in
practice. Leave the DJI rule in `devices[]`, keep everything else in the DSL, and
the two happily coexist.

## When it goes wrong

| What you see | What it usually is |
| --- | --- |
| The receiver never shows up in Sound, Input | Charge-only USB-C cable. Swap it, then try the other port, then turn the receiver off and on while plugged in. |
| Audio only in one ear | The receiver is still in Stereo. Set it to Mono. |
| The transcript is full of nonsense words | Gain too high and distorting, or too low and picking up room noise. Go back to the two-thirds target. |
| You can hear yourself but not them | You're in mic-only mode. Switch to mic plus system audio and grant Screen Recording. |
| You can hear them but not yourself | macOS switched back to the built-in mic, or the call app grabbed the DJI for itself. Reselect the receiver, and set it inside the call app too. |
| The volume popup came back | Karabiner stopped catching it. Check **Modify events** is ticked for the device, and try setting `ignore_vendor_events` to `false`. |
| The button does nothing at all | The transmitter came unpaired, almost always from holding the link button instead of tapping. Hold it on both units to pair them again. |
| It worked, then stopped after you regenerated your config | The rule was in `complex_modifications`. Move it to `devices[]`. |
| The hotkey went back to Fn on its own | Check it with the `defaults export` command above. Don't trust the label. |
| Everything looks right but no text appears | Accessibility permission. The hotkey won't fire without it. |
| The mic cuts out halfway through a meeting | Transmitter battery. You get about five hours. Charge it in the case between meetings. |

## One last thought

There's no driver here, no companion app, nothing DJI ships. The receiver was
already telling your Mac it was a keyboard. All I did was notice, and send one
keypress somewhere more useful.

That happens more than you'd expect. Plenty of USB gadgets send standard
keyboard events that have nothing to do with what they're sold as, and
Karabiner-EventViewer will show you what any of them are really saying. There
might be a button on something you already own that your Mac is quietly willing
to listen to.
